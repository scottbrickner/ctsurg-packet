01

Start here

Almost everything that makes a post-cardiotomy patient behave strangely traces back to one thing: cardiopulmonary bypass. Blood is drained from the vena cava, oxygenated and stripped of CO₂ by a membrane, warmed or cooled by a heat exchanger, and returned to the ascending aorta by a pump. The coagulopathy, the inflammatory storm, the AKI, the delirium, the fluid shifts — those are the pump's fingerprints, not separate diagnoses.

The second thing to internalize is that post-op targets are procedure-specific and sometimes opposite. A myectomy and a CABG in adjacent beds want contradictory things from you. The number that keeps one patient alive will decompensate the other. Most of what follows is built around that one idea, because it is the fastest way an experienced ICU nurse gets into trouble here.

How to use this

This is a survey, not a protocol. Every dose and target here is a reference baseline — the patient's own orders and current institutional policy always win. When the two disagree, the order wins and you should ask why.

02

The first hour

You have taken plenty of admissions. You have probably never taken a fresh heart. The pattern is different enough that it is worth walking once before you do it live.

A post-op cardiac patient arrives intubated, cold, coagulopathic, and on several drips, accompanied by anesthesia and often perfusion. Nothing about the first hour is leisurely, and almost all of it is sequence rather than judgment — which is good news, because sequence is learnable.

What rolls in with them

Lines and access

Arterial line, one or two central lines, sometimes a PA catheter. Foley. Peripheral IVs.

Do firstLevel and zero every transducer to the phlebostatic axis before you believe a single number.

Drainage

One or more chest tubes — mediastinal, pleural, or both. A CSF drain if the repair extended into the descending aorta.

Do firstConfirm suction, confirm the system sits below chest level, and mark the starting output with a time.

Epicardial pacer wires

Atrial and ventricular wires are placed in most cases; either can fail independently. They are the backup rhythm for the next several days.

Do firstCheck capture and sensing thresholds on both. If the patient is already bradycardic, asystolic, or being paced, that’s your answer on dependency — the wires just have to work.

What to get out of the handoff

Anesthesia and perfusion know things about the next twelve hours that are not written anywhere yet. Ask for them before the team scatters.

  • Pump time, cross-clamp time, and — for arch cases — circulatory arrest time. Long runs predict coagulopathy, vasoplegia, and a slower wake-up. This is your single best predictor of how the night goes.
  • What came off the table. The exact operation, not the scheduled one. Plans change intraoperatively, and the hemodynamic targets in Section 4 hinge on which one actually happened.
  • Blood products given, and whether they are still bleeding. Surgical bleeding and coagulopathic bleeding look identical in the canister and are managed completely differently.
  • Rhythm and pacing. Underlying rhythm, whether they are being paced, and at what settings.
  • Anything that surprised the surgeon. A difficult wean from bypass, a friable aorta, a coronary that did not look right — this is the sentence that explains everything that happens at 0300.

The first sixty minutes

  1. Confirm every drip against the order and the concentrationNot just the rate, and not from memory. Concentrations are in Section 7, What's hanging — an infusion that transferred from another pump or another unit is where the errors live.
  2. Labs and filmsCBC, CMP, coagulation panel, ABG with lactate, and troponin. Twelve-lead ECG. Portable chest film to verify tube and line position.
  3. Set the target for this operationBefore you titrate anything, know whether you are protecting a graft at SBP ≤ 140 or a fresh anastomosis at SBP ≤ 120 — and, after a myectomy or TAAA, whether the usual rules are inverted. Section 4 has the table.
  4. Rewarm, and expect the coagulopathy to followCore to 36–37 °C, gradually. Platelet function improves above 34 °C, so some early oozing resolves on its own as the patient warms. Rapid rewarming buys an overshoot you will then have to treat.
  5. Trend the chest tube, don't just read itOutput q15–30 min with the time written down. A single number means nothing; the slope is the finding. Re-op thresholds vary by procedure — verify against your unit’s current order set. The figures in Section 6 are arch-specific and run higher than a standard CABG or valve.
  6. Start thinking about extubationGoal is 4–6 h for a routine CABG or valve, POD 1–2 after arch work. When extubation runs late, the most common cause is sedation that ran longer than planned.

Two things that should stop the routine

Chest tube output that suddenly stops in a patient who was draining — paired with a rising CVP, distended neck veins, or a falling pressure — is tamponade until an echo says otherwise. The tube did not "finish." It clotted.

Hemodynamic collapse in the first hours does not get standard compressions. Emergent resternotomy at the bedside is the intervention, and Section 8 has the sequence. Know where the tray is before your first shift, not during it.

03

What comes off the table

The recurring cases in a CT surgical ICU, and the one thing each is most likely to do to you.

Coronary & valve

Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting

CABG · on-pump or OPCAB

Arrives POD 0 intubated, extubation goal 4–6 h. Find out which conduits they used — LIMA-to-LAD drives long-term survival, and a radial artery changes what hangs.

WatchBleeding (re-op thresholds), post-operative atrial fibrillation (POAF) at 30–40% peaking POD 2–3, MAP ≥ 70, UOP > 0.5 mL/kg/h.

ConduitsRadial artery spasms — expect a vasodilator early and an oral calcium channel blocker at discharge. IMA can spasm, less readily. Saphenous vein does not spasm; no vasodilator is needed to protect it.

Aortic Valve / Root Replacement

AVR · Bentall · VSARR

Tissue, mechanical, or valve-sparing. Anticoagulation depends entirely on which: mechanical = warfarin lifelong; tissue and VSARR = ASA 81 only.

WatchComplete heart block (5–8% need a PPM). Post-Bentall chest pain POD 0–2 = coronary button ischemia until proven otherwise.

Mitral Valve Surgery

MV repair > replacement

Repair preferred in >90% of cases. Sternotomy or robotic. Highest POAF rate of any cardiac surgery at 40–50%.

WatchSAM after repair flips the rules — more volume and afterload, no vasodilators or inotropes. Echo before you treat the hypotension.

Tricuspid Valve Surgery

TV repair · replacement

Usually concomitant with MV or AVR. Isolated TV surgery carries 10–15% mortality. RV recovery runs weeks to months.

WatchTrend the CVP, don't chase a number — accept 12–15 if the patient is perfusing. Avoid aggressive diuresis.

Aortic

Ascending / Arch Open Repair

Hemiarch · total arch · FET

Sternotomy, CPB, and for arch work deep hypothermic circulatory arrest with antegrade cerebral perfusion. The most fragile patients on the unit.

WatchSBP < 120 strictly and neuro exams that stay frequent for 72 h — deficits declare late.

Descending / TAAA / TEVAR

Open thoracotomy or endovascular

Open TAAA carries 5–20% paraplegia risk by Crawford type; TEVAR 2–5%. Spinal cord ischemia here is preventable, which is the whole point.

WatchCSF drain < 10 mmHg, output 10–20 mL/h, MAP ≥ 85, hourly lower-extremity motor and sensory checks.

Rhythm & structural

Cox-MAZE IV

Surgical AF ablation

RF or cryo lesion sets plus LAA ligation, usually concomitant. 70–85% AF-free at one year in paroxysmal AF.

WatchAF in the first 90 days is expected — the blanking period. Rate control and amiodarone, not cardioversion.

Septal Myectomy

HCM · LVOT obstruction

Slow, full, squeezed. Phenylephrine is the pressor. The bundle of His runs through the septum, so heart block is real.

WatchInotropes and vasodilators increase the gradient. Everything you'd reach for post-CABG is wrong here.

TAVR

Transcatheter AVR

No CPB, transfemoral in most cases, POD 1 discharge common. DAPT × 1 month then ASA lifelong.

WatchNew LBBB or CHB needing a PPM in 10–20%. Retroperitoneal bleed: tachycardia + flank pain + Hgb drop = CT stat.

05

Aortic dissection

An intimal tear lets blood dissect into the aortic wall. Where the tear sits determines whether the patient goes to the OR in the next hour or to a bed with an esmolol drip.

Type A — the clock is the diagnosis

Roughly 1–2% mortality per hour untreated across the first 48 hours. When one transfers in from an outside hospital, the goal is the OR within an hour of arrival. Sudden tearing chest or back pain with a pulse or BP differential between arms, or a widened mediastinum, is the presentation. CT angiography is the gold standard at 95–98% sensitivity and specificity; D-dimer is a rule-out tool only.

Sequence matters — beta-blocker first

Impulse control is IV beta-blocker first (esmolol or labetalol, targeting HR ≤ 60), then a vasodilator (nicardipine or nitroprusside, targeting SBP ≤ 120). Never a vasodilator alone: the reflex tachycardia raises dP/dt and propagates the dissection. This is the single most consequential ordering error in aortic care.

  1. Confirm imaging and accessCTA or MRI in hand, two IVs, arterial line, Foley.
  2. Beta-blockade STATLabetalol 5–20 mg IV push, repeating 5–40 mg q5–10 min to a max of 80 mg per dose; or esmolol, loaded then run at 50 mcg/kg/min. Target HR ≤ 60.
  3. Then the vasodilatorNicardipine 5 mg/hr, up by 1 mg/hr q5 min to a max of 15, targeting SBP ≤ 120. Clevidipine 1 mg/hr is the alternative. Only after the rate is controlled.
  4. Type and cross 6+ unitsAnticipate CPB, possible DHCA, and major bleeding.
  5. Mobilize the teamCT surgery, anesthesia, OR — simultaneously, not sequentially. Consent while this runs.

Most of the time you will meet this patient after the repair, not before — but you may hold one briefly while the OR turns over, and the sequence above is what you are maintaining when you do. Post-op 30-day mortality runs 10–15%. Expect ICU POD 4–6+ and a total stay of 10–14+ days, longer and sicker than an elective ascending repair.

Type B — medical management, with conditions

Tear distal to the left subclavian. If there's no malperfusion, no rupture, and no refractory pain, it is uncomplicated and managed medically: strict impulse control at HR ≤ 60 and SBP 100–120, two IVs, arterial line, serial neuro exams and peripheral pulses each shift, repeat imaging at 48–72 h. Complicated Type B — malperfusion, expansion, refractory pain or hypertension — goes to TEVAR. Ninety-day mortality is 10–20% for uncomplicated disease and higher for complicated.

06

Total arch, DHCA, and the frozen elephant trunk

Arch patients are among the sickest post-operative cardiac patients on the unit: prolonged bypass, circulatory arrest, multiple transfusions, and an extended window in which neurologic deficits can still declare.

How far the operation went

Ascending repair

Zone 0 · no arch involvement

Simple tube graft, often with AVR or valve-sparing root replacement. No DHCA needed. Shorter pump time, lower morbidity.

ExpectICU 2–4 days, discharge POD 7–10 if elective.

Hemiarch

Zones 0–1

Stops at or before the left carotid. Requires DHCA plus antegrade cerebral perfusion. Typical circulatory arrest 30–45 min.

ExpectInnominate and left carotid reimplanted; left subclavian variable.

Total arch

Zones 0–2

All three arch vessels reimplanted. Longest arrest times, 40–45 min, and the highest DHCA-related morbidity. Reserved for dissection or extensive aneurysm.

ExpectThe highest neuro-vigilance burden of any case you'll take.

Frozen elephant trunk

FET · staged repair

Hemiarch or total arch with the graft partially deployed into the descending aorta. The "trunk" is a landing zone for a second operation in 6–12 weeks.

ExpectStaging is discharge teaching. If the second operation slips past 3 months, the false lumen can re-expand.

Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest

Three things at once: profound hypothermia (core cooled to 18–22 °C, or moderate 22–28 °C with adjuncts), complete circulatory arrest, and selective cerebral perfusion — antegrade via the axillary or innominate artery, which is the modern standard. Cerebral metabolic rate falls roughly 7% per degree Celsius, so at 18 °C the brain tolerates 30–40 minutes of arrest if rewarming is gradual.

The 72-hour rule

Post-op stroke risk is 3–5% with modern DHCA plus ACP, and 8–10% with older DHCA-only technique. Deficits declare late — over 48 to 72 hours as rewarming completes. The POD 0 exam is unreliable because of residual sedation; your real baseline is POD 1 AM. Do not lower your guard after POD 1, and know the last-normal time.

The post-op arc

POD 0ICU only
  • Neuro q1h × 4 h, then q4h. Focal deficits, lower-extremity strength and sensation, mental status.
  • SBP ≤ 120 strictly — labetalol 5–20 mg push then 5–40 mg q5–10 min, or a nicardipine drip at 5 mg/hr titrated by 1 mg/hr q5 min. MAP ≥ 65 for renal perfusion, but the SBP ceiling takes priority.
  • Don't crash the pressure. Rapid drops in the first hour risk watershed stroke — drift down over 1–2 hours, not 20 minutes.
  • Chest tube output q15–30 min. Re-op territory: > 400 mL/h × 1 h, > 300 × 2–3 h, > 200 × 4 h. Higher baseline output than a CABG — more suture lines.
  • Core temp to 36–37 °C gradually. Hypothermic coagulopathy reverses above 34 °C.
  • Document pacer capture and sensing thresholds now — you'll need the comparison.
POD 1–3ICU → stepdown
  • Extubation goal POD 1–2. Common delays: post-DHCA pulmonary edema, diaphragm dysfunction, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.
  • Neuro vigilance continues. Every shift minimum, q4h preferred through POD 3. Delirium is very common and the hypoactive form is easy to miss.
  • Coagulopathy normalizes POD 2–3 as fibrinogen and platelets recover. Transfuse below Hgb 8, or 9 if age > 70 or known CAD.
  • Complete heart block can appear POD 1–4. Roughly 7–10% of root repairs need permanent pacing.
  • POAF peaks POD 2–3. Replace K⁺ ≥ 4.0 and Mg²⁺ ≥ 2.0 first, then call.
  • Transition SBP control from IV to PO — metoprolol 25–50 mg BID once tolerating diet, bridging off labetalol and nicardipine.
POD 4+stepdown · telemetry
  • Pacer wires stay POD 4–7 — longer than a CABG. Do not remove on the floor without a surgeon or APP order.
  • SBP ≤ 120 remains the target for the first 4–6 weeks after surgery. This is not a soft goal.
  • Bowel regimen is mandatory — straining is Valsalva, and Valsalva is aortic stress on fresh anastomoses.
  • Discharge teaching: lifelong surveillance CTA at 1, 6, and 12 months then annually; no lifting beyond the surgeon's stated limit × 6 weeks; genetic counseling and first-degree relative screening for Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, Ehlers-Danlos, or bicuspid aortopathy.
  • Arch repair without a mechanical valve means aspirin only — not routine warfarin. This is a common misconception.

Three nerves and a cord

Recurrent laryngeal nerve

Hoarseness post-op is RLN injury until proven otherwise. No PO diet without a swallow evaluation. A weak cough means high aspiration risk; NG feeds until it recovers, typically 6–12 weeks for a neuropraxia.

Phrenic nerve

Elevated hemidiaphragm on serial CXR plus unexplained dyspnea or hypoxemia. Usually recovers in 6–12 weeks; plication is an option if severe and persistent. Document it on imaging for future reference.

Spinal cord

If the repair extended into the descending aorta there may be a CSF drain: positioned at the foramen magnum, output 10–20 mL/h, MAP ≥ 85. Any new lower-extremity weakness = STAT call and raise the MAP goal immediately.

Coronary buttons (Bentall)

Not a nerve, but it belongs in the same reflex. Any new chest pain POD 0–2 after a Bentall is a STAT ECG, troponin, and OR standby — button compression or kinking presents as ST elevation and cardiogenic shock.

07

What's hanging

Pressors raise pressure by constricting. Inotropes raise contractility. Inodilators do both, which is why they need a pressor riding alongside. The concentrations and titration parameters below are drawn from an institutional IV infusion titration policy — representative of adult CT surgical practice, but not universal.

Read the columns this way — and check them against yours

Standard concentration is how pharmacy sends it. Initial rate, titration, and maximum are defaults that apply only when the prescriber hasn't specified otherwise — that framing is what lets you titrate without calling for every change. Where is the unit restriction: an asterisk means fixed dose only, no titration, and you may not initiate there — therapy transfers in from ICU once the dose is stable.

Standard concentrations are an institutional decision, not a fact about the drug. Norepinephrine 8 mg/250 mL is common; it is not universal, and neither are the maximums or the unit restrictions. Confirm every one of these against your own pharmacy's chart before you rely on a number here.

Units — this is the thing to unlearn

Institutions split on this, and it is the error most likely to bite you on arrival. In the scheme below, norepinephrine, epinephrine, phenylephrine, nitroglycerin, and isoproterenol all run in mcg/min, not weight-based. The weight-based agents are dobutamine, dopamine, milrinone, nitroprusside, and esmolol in mcg/kg/min, and angiotensin II in ng/kg/min. Find out which convention your unit uses on day one. Reading a norepinephrine rate as weight-based when it isn't — or the reverse — is a large error hiding in a small-looking number.

Every one of these is a vesicant

Norepinephrine, phenylephrine, vasopressin, epinephrine, dobutamine, and dopamine are all vesicants — central line is the expectation. Angiotensin II and nitroprusside are central-line-only in practice.

Peripheral administration is permitted for up to 24 hours at defined concentrations — norepinephrine 32 mcg/mL, phenylephrine 0.16 mg/mL, vasopressin 0.4 units/mL, dopamine 3.2 mg/mL — but only if the IV is at least 20 gauge, less than 24 hours old, at or above the forearm, with good blood return, and the site is visible throughout and assessed at least q2h. You re-assess those conditions at the start of every shift. If the patient is awake, teach them to tell you about pain at the site.

One number to confirm with pharmacy

Source documents disagree on the esmolol ceiling. An infusion titration standard gives a bolus of 0.5 mg/kg over 1 min and a maximum of 300 mcg/kg/min; a more recent IV administration standard gives a load of 500 mcg/kg/min over 1 min, a re-load at every titration, and a maximum of 100 mcg/kg/min. That is a threefold difference in the ceiling. Do not resolve it from a packet — resolve it from your own current policy and the order in front of you.

08

Salvage meds

When escalating catecholamines stops buying pressure, the problem is no longer a dosing problem. It's a pathway problem, and there are drugs for that.

Vasoplegic syndrome after bypass

Distributive shock with a preserved or high cardiac output, low SVR, and MAP under 65 despite adequate volume and norepinephrine above 0.25 mcg/kg/min. Incidence runs 5–25%, peaking POD 0–1. The mechanism is bypass-induced, nitric-oxide-mediated vasodilation that simply overrides the catecholamine response — which is why turning the norepi up stops working.

Earlier is dramatically better

Levin et al. (2009) showed improved mortality when methylene blue was started within 3–4 hours of hypotension onset. Waiting until profound shock — MAP under 50, lactate over 4, organ failure — significantly worsens outcomes. If you're watching a MAP of 60 trend down on escalating pressors, advocate for methylene blue now. Do not accept "let's optimize fluids and pressors for another hour."

The escalation ladder

  1. Fix the electrolytesK⁺ ≥ 4.0, Mg²⁺ ≥ 2.0. They're Na/K-ATPase cofactors and they gate everything downstream.
  2. Norepinephrine — 8 mg/250 mLStart 0.5 mcg/min, up by 0.5 q3–5 min, ceiling 30 mcg/min. Onset 1–2 min, so it's working while anything else is being prepared. Note the ceiling: past roughly 20 mcg/min you're fighting catecholamine resistance and the next dose buys almost nothing.
  3. Add vasopressin — 40 units/100 mL0.02 units/min, up by 0.01 to a max of 0.04. Different receptor, different pathway, and it keeps working in acidosis. Add it once norepi has plateaued rather than after it has failed.
  4. Methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg1% is an IV push over several minutes; ProvayBlue 0.5% is an IVPB over 5–30 min diluted in D5W. Vesicant — central line preferred. Onset 15–30 min; expect a 10–20 mmHg MAP rise if given early.
  5. Angiotensin II (Giapreza) — restricted2.5 mg/500 mL NS only, central line, ICU. Third-line by policy: the patient must already be on norepinephrine and a second pressor at equivalent dose before it can go up. Start 10–20 ng/kg/min, up by 15 q5 min, max 80 ng/kg/min for the first 3 hours then 40.
  6. Stress-dose hydrocortisone 50 mg q6hEspecially if the patient is on chronic steroids at home or the picture looks septic. May be given undiluted, 500 mg or fraction over 1 minute.

Methylene blue

guanylate cyclase inhibitor

Blocks the NO → cGMP → vasodilation cascade at its source rather than trying to out-shout it with more catecholamine. That's why the MAP response is often dramatic when it's given early and gradual when it's late.

AdministrationVesicant — central line preferred. 1% is an IV push over several minutes; ProvayBlue 0.5% is an IVPB over 5–30 min in D5W. Turns urine bright blue-green — warn the patient, family, and night shift before you give it. Stains the tubing. Transient false-low SpO₂ on some monitors; check against clinical perfusion.

Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit)

direct NO scavenger

Binds and inactivates NO rather than blocking the pathway. Less studied in cardiac surgery, more familiar from cyanide toxicity, and more expensive — but no serotonin syndrome risk and no G6PD problem.

Nursing notesTurns urine bright red — warn the patient, family, and night shift, same as you would for methylene blue. Transient creatinine bump from the pigment, and it interferes with colorimetric lab assays for a period after dosing. No serotonin syndrome risk and no G6PD problem, which is exactly why it exists on the shelf.

Before methylene blue — check the MAR

  • G6PD deficiency — oxidizes RBCs, causing hemolysis and renal failure. Hydroxocobalamin instead.
  • MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine, linezolid) — serotonin syndrome. Hydroxocobalamin instead.
  • High-dose SSRIs plus other serotonergic agents — rare at standard dosing but reportable. Flag it to the APP.
  • Cr > 3 or eGFR < 15 — may accumulate; consider 0.5–1 mg/kg with ICU-level monitoring.
  • QT prolongation history — verify an ECG pre-dose if time allows.

Where this happens

Vasoplegia-level support is an ICU problem, not a floor problem. Multiple vasopressors, a continuous arterial line, labs q1–2 h, and methylene blue are ICU infrastructure. If you're escalating beyond simple norepinephrine on a lower-acuity unit, activate the ICU bed rather than trying to titrate through it.

When the escalation runs out

Emergent bedside sternotomy

Acute collapse, PEA or asystolic arrest, or massive chest tube bleeding in a post-op cardiac patient: STAT page CT surgery and activate the open chest code. Target resternotomy within 5 minutes of arrest onset.

Do not start standard external compressions — they risk graft injury and tamponade rupture. Delay 1–2 minutes to let the team assemble for internal cardiac massage. Activate MTP. Bring the sterile resternotomy tray, bovie, suction, and retractor to the bedside. Mortality is 40–60%; the team debrief afterward is not optional.

Emergent ECMO cannulation

When post-cardiotomy shock is refractory to everything above — maximal pressors, inotropes, the NO-pathway agents — or when an arrest doesn't resolve, VA-ECMO is the next step, cannulated emergently at the bedside or back in the OR.

You will not manage the ECMO. Once cannulated, the patient transfers to the ECMO unit and stays there — they do not come back to this unit. That makes your job the two things on either side of the cannulation: recognizing it early enough to be useful, and handing off well.

Have the picture assembled before the receiving team arrives — every pressor with its dose and the time it was started, the lactate trend rather than the last value, the most recent echo, current labs and coagulation studies, and what has already been tried and failed. They are starting from your handoff, and the difference between a good one and a shrug is real. Survival in post-cardiotomy cardiogenic shock runs roughly 40–50%, which is also worth knowing before a family asks you.

Electrolytes, rhythm, and hardware

The unglamorous work that prevents most of the calls you'd otherwise be making at 0300.

Replacement targets

Post-op atrial fibrillation

Thirty to forty percent after CABG, forty to fifty percent after mitral surgery, peaking POD 2–3. New irregular rhythm on tele → 12-lead to confirm, replace K⁺ ≥ 4.0 and Mg²⁺ ≥ 2.0, then notify the APP for a rate-versus-rhythm plan. Unstable — hypotension, chest pain, altered mental status, pulmonary edema — is a STAT call with pads on for synchronized cardioversion. Anticipate amiodarone hypotension in about 25% and back the drip down early rather than late.

Glucose

Post-CPB hyperglycemia is near-universal, insulin-naive patients included — it's the stress and inflammatory response, not undiagnosed diabetes. Persistent glucose above 180 in the ICU means an insulin drip, 0.05–0.1 units/kg/hr titrated q1–2h. Target is 140–180 mg/dL per STS. Tight control is the wrong instinct here: driving below 110 worsens outcomes rather than improving them.

Continuous insulin infusion is ICU and stepdown only, run through a computerized IV insulin dosing and monitoring program rather than titrated by hand. Once they're eating, transition to basal-bolus-correctional and overlap the drip 2–4 hours — never stop a drip and go straight to sliding scale. Expect afternoon spikes from steroids.

Hypoglycemia on a post-op cardiac patient

Under 70 is a Level 1 alert; under 54 with symptoms is clinically important; altered mental status or seizure is emergent. Awake and swallowing: 15–20 g oral glucose, recheck in 15 minutes. Unable to swallow: D50 25 g IV push or glucagon 1 mg IM, recheck q15 min until above 100.

Then find the cause — drip over-titration, a sulfonylurea, AKI slowing insulin clearance, sepsis. And remember the beta-blocker: nearly every patient on this unit is on one, and it masks the adrenergic warning signs. The first thing you may notice is confusion.

Hardware you'll manage

Epicardial pacer wires

Backup rhythm support, typically 3–5 days, longer after aortic work. Check capture and sensing thresholds every shift. Output at 2–3× capture threshold; sensitivity at ≤ half the intrinsic voltage.

Know before you touchIs this patient pacer-dependent? Loss of capture in someone who is = STAT code response with transcutaneous pads at the bedside. Monitor q15 min × 1 h after removal for late tamponade.

Chest tubes

Output and character hourly POD 0–1, q2h if stable. System stays below chest level. Tidaling is normal; continuous bubbling is a leak. Removal at < 150 mL/8 h, no air leak × 24 h, serous, stable.

Red flagSudden cessation of output with a hemodynamic change = tamponade until proven otherwise. STAT echo and call. Gentle milking only; never aggressive stripping.

Sternal precautions

Six to twelve weeks. No pushing or pulling, no overhead reaching, no driving × 4–6 weeks, cough with pillow splinting. The lifting limit is the surgeon's call — generally 5–10 lb after sternotomy, 10 lb after aortic repair. Check the order rather than quoting a number from memory.

Dehiscence red flagsSternal click or pop, grinding, mid-sternal drainage, fever. Most complications land POD 0–3. Mobility within precautions is protective, not risky.

10

Call now

Findings in a currently stable patient that mean the trajectory has changed. None of these are "watch and see."

Who you're calling

The APP first, for most things

Rhythm changes, electrolytes, pain, diuresis, an output trend you don't like, a pressor requirement that's creeping. They are the fastest route to an order and they know these patients daily.

CT surgery for the chest

Bleeding at re-op thresholds, tamponade physiology, sternal instability or drainage, anything involving the graft, the anastomosis, or the wound. Do not route a surgical problem through a medical consult.

Both, plus intensivist, when it's shock

Escalating vasopressor requirement, a MAP you cannot hold, new neuro deficit, arrest. Page in parallel rather than in sequence — the packet's worst-case sections all assume the team assembles at once.

Escalate on a trend, not a threshold. "Norepinephrine has doubled since midnight and the lactate is up" is a better call than waiting for the MAP to break 65.

Neurologic

  • Any new focal deficit, any time — stroke code, and get the last-normal time.
  • New or worsening hoarseness — RLN injury; swallow eval before diet advances.
  • Weak cough with dyspnea — aspiration risk climbing.
  • New lower-extremity weakness with a CSF drain in — STAT call, raise the MAP goal, check drain patency.
  • Increasingly anxious, restless, or unexpectedly drowsy — subtle mental status change often precedes hemodynamic collapse. Don't file it under delirium.

Hemodynamic

  • SBP trending ≥ 130 after arch or dissection repair despite regimen escalation — anastomotic rupture risk. "Close enough" isn't.
  • SBP dropping acutely (120 → 90) in the early hours — watershed stroke risk from overshoot.
  • Rising lactate, falling UOP, cool extremities despite an adequate BP — perfusion failure.
  • Escalating vasopressor requirement POD 2–3 after a long pump run — vasoplegia persisting; discuss ICU re-transfer early.

Bleeding & tamponade

  • Chest tube output not trending down after POD 1, or rising again POD 2+.
  • Sudden drop in output with rising CVP or JVD and hypotension — tamponade physiology. STAT echo.
  • Post-Bentall chest pain POD 0–2 — coronary button ischemia. ECG, troponin, OR standby.

Rhythm & infection

  • New bradycardia under 55 with symptoms — delayed complete heart block; prepare transcutaneous pacing.
  • Pacer capture threshold rising — early warning of loss of capture.
  • QTc approaching 500 ms on amiodarone — torsades risk.
  • Sternal instability with fever — DSWI. Cultures before antibiotics, CT surgery STAT.
  • Persistent fever POD 2+ without a source in a graft patient — consider graft infection. Blood cultures ×2, CTA, ID and CT surgery.
